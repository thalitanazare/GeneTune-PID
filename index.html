GeneTune-PID

PID tuning and genetic algorithms on an 8-bit microcontroller · ball-in-tube levitator Replay mode · measured data set (10 tunings, 430 trials)

Start here · What GeneTune-PID is and how to use it

What this platform does GeneTune-PID teaches how to tune a PID controller for a real plant, first with a classical rule (Ziegler–Nichols) and then with a genetic algorithm (GA) that runs inside an 8-bit microcontroller. All curves come from measured experiments on the rig, except the model simulations on this page and in Stage 2. Read this page once, try the simulator, then follow the stages in order.

Try it: the ball-in-tube plant under PID control (model simulation)

The ball starts at the bottom of the tube (h ≈ 31 cm) and the controller must lift it to 15 cm. Move a slider and press Play: the drawing of the rig follows the curve in time. Try Ki = 0 (the ball settles away from 15 cm) or a large Kp (more overshoot).

1 · The plant

A fan at the bottom of a vertical tube blows air upwards and lifts a light ball. An ultrasonic sensor at the top measures the distance h from the sensor to the ball every T = 0.1 s. The Arduino sets the fan speed with a PWM command u between 0 and 255.

More airflow lifts the ball, so h decreases when u increases. The task is to hold the ball at the setpoint r = 15 cm.

2 · The PID controller

At every sample k the controller computes the error and three corrective terms, in the discrete parallel form:

ek = s (r − hk), uk = Kpek + KiT Σj≤k ej + Kd(ek − ek−1)/T
  • P reacts to the present error: faster response, more overshoot.
  • I accumulates past error: removes the steady offset.
  • D reacts to the rate of change of the error: adds damping.

Because h falls when u rises, the error sign is inverted: s = −1.

3 · Measuring performance

Each experiment is a 35-s trial: 5 s with the fan off, then a 30-s step to r = 15 cm. Its cost is the ITAE (integral of time-weighted absolute error), in cm·s²:

J = T² Σk k |ek|

Each error is multiplied by its time, so slow settling is penalised more than the unavoidable initial error. Lower J is better. The platform also reports overshoot, settling time and the actuator variation TV = Σ|uk − uk−1|, which measures how hard the fan works.

4 · Tuning with a genetic algorithm

A GA searches for good gains (Kp, Ki, Kd) by imitating natural evolution:

  • a population of Np candidate gain sets is tested, one rig trial each;
  • the fitness of each candidate is its measured ITAE;
  • better candidates are selected, combined by crossover and slightly changed by mutation;
  • the best candidate (elite) is kept unchanged, and the cycle repeats for G generations.
trials = Np + G(Np − 1) + 1

Each trial takes 35 s on the rig, so the number of trials, not computation, limits the search.

5 · Noise and confirmation

Repeating the same gains on the rig gives a different ITAE each time, because of sensor noise and turbulent airflow. When the GA keeps the lowest of many noisy measurements, that stored score tends to be lucky (optimistic). An independent confirmation trial of the winner gives a realistic cost. This optimistic bias of noisy fitness evaluation is a known problem in evolutionary optimisation.

To save time, a trial is stopped early once its running cost exceeds twice the best stored cost: the cost can only grow, so that trial can no longer become the best.

Stage 1 · Relay test and Ziegler–Nichols tuning

Bloom level: Apply
Goal: obtain a first set of PID gains from one experiment In the relay test the fan switches between two levels (an on–off controller), so the ball oscillates around 15 cm. The size and period of this oscillation describe the plant, and the Ziegler–Nichols (ZN) rule turns them into gains. The relay test, proposed by Åström and Hägglund, estimates the ultimate gain Ku = 4d/(πa) by the describing-function approximation.
  1. Look at the relay plot: the green arrow shows the peak-to-valley distance 2a, and the purple arrow shows the period Pu. Their values are in the two coloured boxes below the plot.
  2. Enter a and Pu in the boxes, compute Kp, Ki and Kd with the formulas, enter your gains and press Check.
  3. Then watch the measured response with the ZN gains at the bottom of the page.

Stage 2 · Tuning as optimisation on an identified model

Bloom level: Understand
Goal: see what each PID term does and what the ITAE cost rewards A GA does not "know" control: it only compares the cost of each gain set. Here you play the GA's role on a computer model of the rig, so every change is evaluated instantly instead of with a 35-s rig trial.
  1. Run the five guided experiments below in order. Each one sets the gains and tells you what to look for in the curve and in the numbers.
  2. Then move the sliders freely and try to beat ZN: the green or red box on the right compares your ITAE with ZN.

Guided experiments

Simulated response (solid) and ZN reference (dashed)

Slider ranges are the GA search box [0, 2θZN]: each gain may vary from zero to twice its ZN value. The model was identified from rig data: the ball velocity responds to the fan command with a first-order lag (time constant τb = 2.7 s), and the position integrates the velocity (τbdx/dt = −x + u − u0, dh/dt = −Kbx, with u0 ≈ 110 PWM the command that keeps the ball still).

Closed-loop cost of the current gains

ITAE weights late errors by time, so slow settling is penalised. TV is the total variation of the actuator, Σ|uk − uk−1|.

This model omits sensor noise and changes in the physical rig. Its actuator-variation ranking can differ from measured trials; use the rig data to check the prediction.

Side calculation: the cost of a GA on a PC

This box does not change the simulation. It counts how many model simulations a GA on a computer needs: Np candidates in each of G + 1 generations, each simulated R times with different noise and its ITAE averaged. Compare with the on-chip GA of Stage 3, where every evaluation is a 35-s rig trial.

Stage 5 · Designing a retuning rule

Bloom level: Create
The situation The ball has been tuned to float at 15 cm. The Arduino now runs the plant for a long time, in episodes of 30 s, and after each episode it scores how well the controller did: the episode cost Jn (higher = worse). At some point an operator moves the setpoint to 24 cm. The old gains were tuned for 15 cm, so the control gets worse and the cost jumps.
The Arduino must notice this by itself and start a new GA tuning. It does so with an alarm level: if an episode costs more than γ times the cost of the first episode, it retunes. Your job is to choose γ, like setting the sensitivity of a smoke alarm. Comparing a running cost with a reference value is the basic idea of controller performance monitoring:
  • too sensitive (γ small): it goes off with normal noise at 15 cm and starts a useless 25-min tuning;
  • too insensitive (γ large): it stays silent when the setpoint really changes.
